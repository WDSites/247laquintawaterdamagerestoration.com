What Griffin Ranch is

Griffin Ranch is a residential neighborhood in La Quinta, described in public references as an equestrian-oriented resort neighborhood developed with the help of Merv Griffin. Toll Brothers announced a gated community of 37 single-story homes at Griffin Ranch in June 2025, with a clubhouse, pool and mountain views.

That is what we can verify. We do not have official boundaries, a home count for the whole neighborhood or a construction timeline, so we do not give them.

What this means for water damage

Newer homes are not immune to leaks. Supply lines, water heaters, appliance hoses, air conditioner drains and roof penetrations can fail at any age. Newer construction also tends to be tighter, which means moisture stays inside a wall cavity until someone looks.

A single-story house changes where water goes. A leak in an attic line, a roof penetration or an air conditioning drain wets the ceiling and then the floor below it, while a leak from a fixture spreads across one level. We do not claim that this neighborhood has more or fewer losses than others.

Rain, runoff and flood maps

The city's storm safety page recorded a local emergency and a heavy sandbag distribution during Tropical Storm Hilary in August 2023, which was a citywide event. It does not say Griffin Ranch flooded, and we do not either. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified. Check an address on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center.

If a storm does bring water to a door or low point, see our page on flood damage restoration and treat floodwater as contaminated.

First-hour safety steps

Close the shutoff valve at the fixture, or the main water valve. Do not touch outlets or switch breakers while standing in water, and keep people and pets out of water that came from a drain. If a ceiling is bulging, stay clear, and for sparks or a gas smell leave and call the utility or emergency services.

Photograph the source and the damage, lift valuables, and avoid ordinary vacuums on wet floors.

Extraction, drying and insurance notes

Crews extract water, map moisture with meters and a thermal camera, remove materials that cannot dry and run air movers and dehumidifiers until readings reach the dry standard in ANSI/IICRC S500. Finished surfaces such as stone, engineered flooring and built-in cabinets can need extra care, and a crew should explain what can be dried in place.

Ask your insurer what your policy covers and ask your HOA about its rules. Photos, readings and an itemized scope help describe the damage but do not guarantee coverage.

Do newer homes have fewer water problems?

Who should I call first, my HOA or my insurer?

Stop the water and photograph it first. Then notify your HOA if shared areas are involved and your insurer about coverage.

Can finished flooring be saved?

Sometimes. Dense stone dries differently from engineered wood, and readings decide. A crew can explain what can stay.
