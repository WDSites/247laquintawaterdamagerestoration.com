How we work

Every job follows the same order. Stop the source, remove the water, dry the structure and measure the result, then repair. We explain the scope and how the price is set before work starts, and we document the damage in writing so you can share it with your insurer.

We follow the published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration and S520 for mold remediation, and we encourage you to ask any company you hire whether its crews do the same. Read more about the services we offer.

What a typical call sounds like

Dispatch starts with safety, not a sales script. Is anyone in danger, is the water near outlets or the electrical panel, and has the main valve been shut off? Those answers decide whether the first instruction is to leave the room, cut power if the panel is dry and safe to reach, or simply lift valuables off the floor.

Then come the questions that shape the crew and equipment. Where is the water coming from, which rooms are wet, how long has it been wet, and what are the floors and walls made of? Tile over slab behaves very differently from carpet over pad, and a closet shared with an air handler is a different problem from a bathroom ceiling.

Photos and short videos help a great deal, so if you can take them safely, do it before anything is moved. They give the crew a picture of the loss and give you a record for your insurer.

The standards and sources we point to

We lean on published sources rather than our own say-so. For water damage that means the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, which defines clean, gray and black water and sets out how structures are dried. For mold it means S520 and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's guidance, which says mold needs moisture and that drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours usually prevents growth.

On insurance we point to the California Department of Insurance, which states that standard homeowners policies do not cover flood and that flood coverage is a separate policy, most often through the National Flood Insurance Program. For permits we send people to the City of La Quinta Building Division, because repair work on a structure generally needs a permit before it starts.

None of that makes us your insurer, your building department or your physician. It means that when we tell you something, you can check it against the source.

Why the east valley needs its own playbook

A desert home fails differently from a coastal or northern one. La Quinta gets under 4 inches of rain in a normal year, so a leak behind a wall can stay damp for weeks without a visible sign, and summer afternoons regularly top 100 degrees, which keeps air conditioners running and their condensate lines busy. Many homes sit empty for part of the year, which lets small leaks run unseen.

Then there are the rare storms. Tropical Storm Hilary in August 2023 put two-day rain totals of 3.82 inches in Palm Desert and 2.35 inches at Thermal Airport, according to National Weather Service figures reported at the time. That is close to a normal year of rain falling on hard, dry ground, and it is why we treat runoff and flood response as a real part of the work.
